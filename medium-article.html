The break reminder I actually take

Most of them nag on a wall clock. I wanted one that only counts the time I was really at the screen - so I built it for my Mac, in about 3,500 lines of Swift and no dependencies.

I have installed and deleted a lot of break reminders. They fail in one of two ways, and both failures are about trust.

The first kind nags on a dumb wall clock. It fires in the middle of a meeting. It fires thirty seconds after I sit down from lunch, having counted the entire lunch as heads-down screen time. After a week of being wrong at exactly the wrong moment, I stop reading it.

The second kind is so easy to dismiss that dismissing it becomes a reflex. A banner slides in, my thumb swats it away, and I could not tell you what it said. It is technically installed. It is doing nothing.

So I wrote Downtime: a macOS menu bar app that tries to earn the interruption before it takes one. It looks after the three things a long day at a computer quietly costs you - your eyes, your back, and your hydration - and the whole design brief was be right often enough that I don't start ignoring you.

What it looks like

It lives next to the clock and nowhere else. No Dock icon, no window on launch, no account. 

menu bar:    👁 20m        ← next reminder, counting down
click:       dashboard - rings, toggles, water tracker, streak
right-click: break now · pause · settings · quit

Three reminders run on independent timers, each of which can be switched off on its own:

  • Stand & Stretch - every hour, a 3-minute break: stand up, roll your shoulders, actually walk a few steps.
  • Drink Water - every 45 minutes, 30 seconds: refill, and drink it.
  • Rest Your Eyes - every 20 minutes, 20 seconds: look about 20 feet away and blink.

That last one is the optometrist's 20-20-20 rule, and it ships as a one-click preset, because it is the piece of desk-health advice with the best ratio of benefit to effort. Screens roughly halve your blink rate; hours at one fixed focal distance is what leaves your eyes dry and sore by 6pm. Twenty seconds of looking out of a window fixes a surprising amount of it.

When a timer runs out, you get a card - not a banner. It says which break, what to do, and a ring counts the break itself down. Snooze, skip, or let it finish on its own.

Rule one: count screen time, not clock time

This is the whole app, really. A timer that ticks while you are away from your desk is a timer that will be wrong every single day.

So the tick loop asks the system how long the human has been idle, and the answer costs no permissions at all - no Accessibility prompt, no Screen Recording prompt, nothing for the user to grant: 

static func idleSeconds() -> TimeInterval {
    var smallest = TimeInterval.greatestFiniteMagnitude
    for type in inputEvents {
        let value = CGEventSource.secondsSinceLastEventType(
            .combinedSessionState, eventType: type
        )
        if value >= 0 && value < smallest { smallest = value }
    }
    return smallest == .greatestFiniteMagnitude ? 0 : smallest
}

It takes the minimum across key presses, modifier changes, mouse moves, clicks, drags and scrolls, because any one of those on its own lies. Watch a long video and your keyboard has been idle for forty minutes while you were sitting right there.

From that one number, plus screen-lock and display-sleep notifications, three behaviours fall out:

  • Briefly idle (90 seconds by default) - the timers hold. Nothing advances, nothing fires.
  • Away long enough to count (5 minutes by default) - that was a break. It is credited to your stats, every timer resets, and there is a 30-second grace period so the app is not shouting at you the moment you touch the trackpad.
  • Back at the keyboard - counting resumes from where it paused.

The screen being locked has to last just as long as walking away from the keyboard does, so locking your Mac to grab a coffee is not gamed into a free break either.

Coming back from lunch to an instant reminder is the single fastest way to teach someone to ignore your app.

Rule two: know when to stay quiet

Being right also means shutting up on purpose.

There are optional work hours - weekdays plus a time window, with overnight windows handled properly, so a 22:00–06:00 shift does not confuse it into silence. There is a pause button with real durations rather than a vague toggle, including "until tomorrow", and a manual resume that deliberately expires when the schedule next comes around, so an override never quietly leaks into the next day.

And there is fullscreen detection, which is the most interesting one because it is a guess. macOS will not simply tell an unprivileged app "someone is presenting right now", so the app infers it: does any other process own a window whose bounds match a display exactly? 

for size in sizes {
    if abs(rect.width - size.width) < 2 &&
       abs(rect.height - size.height) < 2 {
        return true
    }
}

That catches Keynote, Zoom's shared screen and a fullscreen video. It also catches a maximised window that just happens to fit. Because it is a heuristic and heuristics are occasionally wrong in ways users cannot debug, it is off by default, it says plainly what it does in Settings, and when it is on and holding the timers, the dashboard tells you that is why nothing is counting. A quiet app that will not explain its silence is a broken app.

Rule three: one moment, one interruption

Three timers on 20-, 45- and 60-minute intervals collide constantly. Handled naively you get an eye break, then a water break ninety seconds later, then a stretch - which is not three reminders, it is one long annoyance.

So when several reminders are due in the same second, the most overdue one wins and shows a single card, and the others are marked as served - but only if the break that is actually running is long enough to cover them. A 20-second eye break does not get to absorb your 3-minute stretch. Nothing you dismissed is allowed to reappear thirty seconds later.

Rule four: say what to do, with real numbers

"Time for a break" is a notification. "You've been at the screen for 1 hr 12 min - stand up and reach" is an instruction, and instructions get followed.

Every reminder's headline and body are editable, with {duration}, {minutes}, {time} and {count} placeholders that are filled in from your actual session - so you never see a raw {duration} staring back at you, which is a bug I have shipped before and now check for on purpose.

Snooze exists, because sometimes you genuinely are mid-sentence. It defaults to 10 minutes and is capped at three per break, so it cannot quietly become the way you use the app.

It keeps score, and the score stays on your machine

Habits need a visible surface, so the dashboard shows breaks taken, skipped and snoozed, break time, screen time, glasses of water, a 7-day bar chart and a streak. History is kept for 120 days.

All of it is a JSON file in ~/Library/Application Support/, and settings are plain user defaults. There is an "Erase all history" button that erases all history.

There is no network code in the app at all. Not a disabled analytics SDK, not a telemetry flag defaulting to off, not an update check - nothing that could ever send anything anywhere. For a tool whose entire dataset is "when this person is and isn't at their computer", that felt less like a feature and more like the minimum honest position.

The shape of the code

About 3,500 lines of Swift, zero third-party dependencies, and one idea holding it together: a single AppModel - an ObservableObject driven by a one-second timer - plus three thin AppKit controllers that render it. The menu bar item, the break overlay and the settings window all read the same state and own none of it.

Everything interesting therefore lives in one tick(), which reads top to bottom in the order the questions actually matter: is a break already on screen, are we paused, are we inside work hours, has the person been away long enough to credit, are they merely idle, is something fullscreen - and only then, is anything due? Bugs in an app like this are almost always ordering bugs, and a single flat tick makes the ordering something you can read instead of something you have to reconstruct.

The countdown in the menu bar is set in monospaced digits so it does not jitter as it ticks, which took ten minutes and is the change I notice most. And when the menu bar item resizes while the dashboard is open - a countdown collapsing to a bare paused icon - the popover re-anchors itself instead of being left stranded a few pixels off its item. Nobody will ever file that bug. It just looks wrong until it is fixed.

Building it

You need macOS 13 or later and the Xcode Command Line Tools - not full Xcode. 

git clone https://github.com/pratik-adh/take-a-break.git
cd take-a-break
./build.sh --install

That compiles in release mode, wraps the binary in Downtime.app, turns the 1024px source art into an .icns, ad-hoc signs it, moves it to /Applications and launches it. Releases add --universal for an arm64 + x86_64 binary; plain builds stay native because iterating is much faster that way.

Because it is ad-hoc signed rather than notarized, macOS will warn you on first launch - right-click the app, then Open.

Does it work?

On the only metric I care about: I have had it running for weeks and I have not deleted it, which is more than I can say for anything else in this category. I take the eye breaks almost every time, because twenty seconds of looking out of a window costs nothing. I skip more stretch breaks than I would like to admit, and the stats tell me so, which is its own kind of useful.

The lesson generalises past break reminders, I think. An app that interrupts you is spending your attention, and it has to be right about the moment or it goes bankrupt fast. Almost all of the work here went into knowing when not to speak.
